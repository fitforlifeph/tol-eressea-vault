##### This is an edited version of a [lecture delivered at St John’s University](https://www.stjohns.edu/news-media/news/2026-09-24/how-love-world-vincentian-chair-explores-human-dignity-through-two-extraordinary-lives), New York, in September 2026 as part Professor Rowlands’s year-long appointment as [Vincentian Chair in Social Justice](https://www.stjohns.edu/who-we-are/faith-mission-ministry/vincentian-center-church-and-society/vincentian-chair-social-justice), for which she gives four public lectures to university students, staff and the local community.

In the summer of 1940, two women left Paris, neither of them voluntarily. Although they had never met, they had shared the city as home for seven years. One had lived there all her life; the other had fled there in 1933 to escape the Nazis. Both were secular Jews and outstanding women philosophers of their generation. Simone Weil was French, while Hannah Arendt was born and raised in Germany. Arendt was the older of the two by three years, and by the outbreak of the war in 1939 both women were established young scholars in their early thirties.

As the threat from Germany grew critical in the spring of 1940, Hannah Arendt received notice that all German-born women in Paris were to report to the Vélodrome d’Hiver in the south of the city to be transported to an internment camp in southern France. The camp, Gurs, had just finished being used for the last of those interned after the Spanish Civil War, and the women arrived to discover that the previous inmates had sewn messages into the sacks they would sleep on. Arendt would spend five weeks in the camp, and only in the brief chaos following the Nazi occupation of Paris in June 1940 was she able to escape with forged release papers.

The woman who had cleverly spearheaded this mass escape, [Lisa Fitkko](https://jewishstudies.washington.edu/digital-jewish-studies/lisa-fittko/), would subsequently use her own escape to guide fleeing exiles along perilous mountain routes into Spain and onward to the Americas. We will meet her again later in this story.

Simone Weil, for her part, left Paris just a month after Arendt. On 13 June, the day before Paris fell to the Germans, the Weil family hustled their way onto one of the last trains south, persuading the guards that the mass exodus required the presence of a medical doctor, Weil’s father.

Over the next three years, these two women would travel the same refugee trails across Europe. Repeatedly uprooted, they inhabited the same cities, shared the same perils and risks, did not meet yet knew people in common, and ultimately faced very different fates. As [Roberto Esposito](https://fordhampress.com/the-origin-of-the-political-hb-9780823276264.html) notes, theirs is the sign of a missed encounter. I want to begin by attempting to map that missed encounter, its overlaps with Catholic resistance and its eventual relationship to New York City.

By July 1940, two months after leaving Paris for the South, Hannah Arendt and Simone Weil were living about four hundred kilometres apart. At this point, the stories of both women intersect with that of the Catholic community thrown into wartime resistance.

When Arendt left Gurs, she walked about seventy kilometres to the Marian shrine town of Lourdes, near the Pyrenees. In place of the usual flow of Catholic pilgrims, now halted by war, was a growing community of predominantly Jewish refugees, for whom Lourdes had become a political sanctuary. This was, for example, the origin of Franz Werfel’s promise, made at the grotto during his own extended displacement to Lourdes, that if he and Alma Mahler should reach safety, he would create a tribute to Saint Bernadette, which became the famous novel and film, [*The Song of Bernadette*](https://secure.stpauls.com.au/strathfield/song-of-bernadette-the.html). Arendt had walked to Lourdes hoping to find her friend, the philosopher Walter Benjamin, whom she had last seen in Paris. Reunited, the two managed the stress of their displacement with hours of conversation, chain smoking and chess.

After several weeks in Lourdes, and to the disappointment of Benjamin, Arendt set out once again on foot, this time two hundred kilometres north-east to find friends who could shelter her in Montauban. Montauban had become a centre for hosting refugees thanks to the collaboration of the local leftist mayor, Fernand Balès, and the city’s Catholic bishop, Pierre-Marie Théas.

Théas used his residence to help manage refugee transit, hosting a document-forging unit operated by Jewish organisations under the oversight of his Catholic diocesan secretary, Marie-Rose Gineste. He ordered all Catholic institutions to open their doors to host and hide Jewish refugees, including parishes, convents and schools.

In 1942, Théas authored a pastoral letter condemning the rounding up of Jews and French complicity in antisemitism. His secretary, Marie-Rose, played a critical role in distributing the pastoral letter to each parish. To avoid interception, she cycled it through the late-summer heat at considerable personal risk to every parish in the diocese. She also smuggled the letter north into the hands of resistance figures, who were able to get it to London for broadcast on BBC *Ici Londres* into occupied France. On 30 August 1942, the letter was read from the pulpit of French parishes across the South. This was later credited as a watershed moment for French Catholic resistance. It stated, among other things:

I give voice to the outraged protest of Christian conscience and I proclaim … that all men, whatever their race or religion, have the right to be respected by individuals and by states.


As an aside, in June 1944, Théas was arrested by the Gestapo after delivering a fierce anti-Nazi sermon, and imprisoned. He used this time to minister to his fellow detainees, reminding them that Christians are required to love their enemies and pray for them, noting, to the disgust of some, that this included their Gestapo guards. After the war, Théas became a co-founder of *Pax Christi* and a lifelong campaigner for peace. Théas is one of only three serving French bishops honoured at [Yad Vashem](https://www.yadvashem.org/righteous/stories/theas.html) as “Righteous Among the Nations”. All of which is a long way of saying that Arendt and her husband, Heinrich Blücher, whom she had managed near miraculously to re-find standing in front of her in street in the south of France, would shelter safely with friends for six months in the Montaubon of Théas and Balès.

Meanwhile, Simone Weil had settled in Marseille and discovered the remarkable Dominican community there. She had grown up in an avowedly secular Jewish household and chose not to see herself as Jewish. However, she understood that the regime viewed her as Jewish, and she wrote to the French administration, strongly contesting the imposition of this identity:

If there is a religious tradition that expresses my patrimony, it is the Catholic tradition … The Christian, French, Hellenic tradition is mine; the Hebrew tradition is foreign to me; no text of a law can change that ...


Weil had chosen — in many ways problematically — to reject “the Hebrew tradition” almost in its entirety. Over the course of the war years, she would move further toward a fuller embrace of the Catholic tradition, but without baptism, or at least *probably* without baptism.

In Marseille, through an old Parisian Catholic friend, Weil was introduced to Father Joseph-Marie Perrin, and the two formed an extraordinary friendship between 1941 and 1942. He showed Weil a patience and insight that few others she approached for spiritual guidance did. Perrin also assisted in organising shelter for Jews, smuggling them through church networks, and publishing the resistance journal *Cahier du Témoignage Chrétien*. It should also be noted that when Walter Benjamin climbed the mountain pass to Spain, following Lisa Fitkko’s routes, and tragically committed suicide when intercepted at a closed border, his belongings included a letter from the prior of the Marseille Dominicans that would have enabled sanctuary in a Spanish monastery. It is also thanks to Perrin that many letters and some of Weil’s notebooks survived the war to be posthumously published, including the vastly influential [*Waiting for God*](https://www.routledge.com/Waiting-for-God/Weil/p/book/9780367705282).

During 1940–1941, it became clear to both Arendt and Weil that the south of France could not hold out. Passage to the United States was the best hope. For Arendt, the journey was eventually facilitated by papers from her first husband, Günther Anders, and by being placed on Varian Fry’s priority list. After anxious months of waiting for papers, and unlike poor Walter Benjamin, she travelled legally into Spain and onwards to Lisbon, where she waited several months for a ship. Carrying the precious cargo of a carbon copy of Benjamin’s posthumous manuscript, “Theses on the Philosophy of History”, which she read aloud to passengers on board the ship, Arendt sailed from the most westerly tip of Europe on 10 May 1941, arriving at Ellis Island, New York on 22 May.

The Weil family’s passage was arranged by Andre Weil, Simone’s brilliant mathematician brother, who had already settled in the United States. In Weil’s case, the delay in leaving was primarily psychological. She believed that leaving France was an abandonment and abdication of her wartime responsibilities. She struck a deal with herself: she would accept leaving for the United States to take her parents to safety, and then she would find a way to return to Europe and the resistance effort.

The Weil family sailed from Marseille to Casablanca on 17 May, in the company of a large group of central European rabbis. The fleeing party was delayed on its onward passage to New York by a necessary seventeen days in the Aïn Seba refugee camp in North Africa. Weil famously commandeered the only stool in the camp to sit and write for hours. The other residents presumed she was a journalist sending urgent texts home. In fact, she was working on a text on Pythagoras and some notes on the question of baptism. This tale, later related by her parents, somehow epitomises Simone Weil. The Weils arrived on Ellis Island in early July 1942.

<sup>Simone Weil’s pass for when she worked for the French resistance in 1943. (Photo12 / Universal Images Group via Getty Images)</sup>

This, in outline, is how both Hannah Arendt and Simone Weil became co-residents of yet another city, New York, a year apart — a city in which they would again not meet. This is how they came to play a part in New York’s history, and how the city came to shape their own histories. New York was Arendt’s home for 34 years, until her death at 370 Riverside Drive in Manhattan on 4 December 1975. For ten of those New York years, Arendt was stateless, gaining American citizenship in 1951. Overall, she spent eighteen of her adult years writing, thinking and teaching as a stateless scholar. She not only wrote *about* statelessness but also wrote *out* of it.

By contrast, Weil’s stay in New York lasted just four months, but they were nonetheless formative. She arrived and hit a wall of lethargy and possibly depression, and she may have contracted tuberculosis during these months. Her writing, which had been prolific in Marseille, slowed greatly. Yet New York opened Weil to new experiences and ways of thinking. It was especially important for deepening her engagement with Catholicism and for her immersion in thinking about race and in experiencing African American life.

Weil moved between attending Mass at the simple Franciscan church of Corpus Christi on West 121st Street — the same church that had drawn Thomas Merton in 1938 and where he was baptised in 1939 — and attending services at an unnamed Black Baptist church in Harlem, seemingly on 125th Street. She reported finding a deep sense of peace in the simplicity of Franciscan worship. Equally, she was deeply moved by Black church worship, feeling a depth of faith and joy she had not previously known. We know that at least once she also attended a synagogue frequented by the Black Jewish community, probably the only time she attended a synagogue in her life.

In New York, Weil also sought spiritual counsel and continued to wrestle with the question of baptism. Initially, she turned to the Jesuits at Fordham, with whom, to understate the matter, she did not form the same bond as she had with the Dominican Father Perrin. She wrote rather sharply that she felt the Jesuits she met were passing her between them without much interest, and that the prevailing sentiment was, “Isn’t it time for lunch yet?” So, she gave up on them and, with the assistance of Jacques Maritain, found her way back to the Dominicans, to Father Edouard Couturier OP, with whom she continued to explore her questions. These encounters in New York formed the basis for her [*Letter to a Priest*](https://www.routledge.com/Letter-to-a-Priest/Weil/p/book/9780415267670), published posthumously and intended as a private exchange.

After weeks of pleading with every contact she had in New York, England and France for a route to London and the Free French network, Weil secured a place on a boat bound for Liverpool, England. She sailed in November 1942, fulfilling the promise she had made to herself in Marseille. After reaching London, she lived for only nine more months, though they were prolific.

<sup>Hannah Arendt as a teenager. (Photo by Mondadori via Getty Images)</sup>

Why have I chosen to focus on the lives of these two women? Because I believe they are resonant, adjacent thinkers who help both trouble our categories and clarify why a specifically Catholic social vision has something of value to offer to our own moment. It helps that, in both cases, Arendt and Weil are unorthodox thinkers, theologically and politically. They are unorthodox in their Judaism and in their engagement with Christian sources and ideas. They are heterodox as political thinkers in a liberal era. They use theological ideas rescripted for their own purposes, propose political ideas that do not cohere with any other single school of thought, belong to no canon and cannot find a place to think within any single testament from the past. Yet each knew that to build a future worthy of our human dignity — which both believed in profoundly — we still have to work with the pearls we can gather from that past.

Hope for the future requires the capacity to discern what we should conserve from the past and to remember who we are created to be. Memory is indispensable to hope. Our capacity to create new beginnings is rooted in our capacity to recall our origins, which also speak of our destiny. As Arendt noted pithily, to be human and to create “worlds” rather than merely inhabit a universe is to think in three tenses: past, present, future. All social action that fosters human dignity thinks in three tenses, and resists those things that fundamentally break these threads.

Both Arendt and Weil are also restless thinkers who refuse easy constructions of love, truth and justice. They challenge us to account for the belonging and non-belonging in our accounts of neighbour love, the borders of our thinking.

<sup>Pope Leo XIV shakes hands with Anthropic co-founder Christopher Olah, with Catholic theologian Anna Rowlands standing alongside, at the presentation of “Magnifica Humanitas”, his first encyclical, at the Vatican’s Aula Nuova del Sinodo on 25 May 2026. (REUTERS / Yara Nardi)</sup>

Of course, Hannah Arendt also creeps into the pages of Pope Leo’s [*Magnifica Humanitas*](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html). Leo spots the affinity between aspects of Arendt’s thought and his own call for attention to the category of the human. The plea is for the development of an integral humanism for an age of ecological, political, technological and economic precarity. The urgency of refocusing our attention on the nature, value and unique vocation of the human person resounds throughout Leo’s text.

We cannot protect our planet, our future, our constitutional arrangements, our international norms without a return to the question of the human. Needless to say, this carries the risk of repeating past “humanistic” mistakes, and it would be foolish to reinvent “the human” in our times without recognising, as both Arendt and Weil did, that “the human” has too often meant a particular construction of white, autonomous, Enlightenment man. This has generally been bad news for the “other” of these categories as well as our relationship to other creatures and nature.

*Magnifica Humanitas* seeks to negotiate these risks, presenting the fragility of all dimensions of our ecology alongside the grandeur of our calling as key notes of the document, and proposing a relational, embodied human subject. Central to its act of discernment is the question of *power*. It is therefore not entirely a surprise that Arendt creeps into its pages, nor that someone well known to Arendt from the Catholic tradition, [Romano Guardini](https://archive.org/details/powerandresponsi027468mbp), does too, posing his characteristic dilemma that human beings have not been trained to use power well. Much of what we live with is the manifestation of a crisis in the responsible use of power, in awareness of its source, its purpose, its limits.

At the age of 16, Hannah Arendt, having been expelled from school in Königsberg for challenging a teacher, arrived in Berlin and found herself in Guardini’s classroom. From him she learned to read Søren Kierkegaard and to think through the crisis of modernity. Guardini is a fascinating bridge — not the only one, Jacques Maritain and others too — between the Catholic social tradition, including the current papacy, and Arendt and Weil.

<sup>Italian philosopher and theologian Romano Guardini (1885–1968) in 1960. (Photo by ullstein bild / ullstein bild via Getty Images)</sup>

Arendt and Weil are two figures who thought deeply about the themes of humanity and responsible power and resistance to technocratic modernity that are so central to Pope Leo’s encyclical. And that is embodied, earthed thinking that migrated from Berlin to Paris to Marseille to New York to London, and we might say, to Rome, tying our continents in the attempt to think our times — a tying that much of our own moment risks being untied.

Hannah Arendt and Simone Weil disagreed at least as much as they could be said to agree. However, I believe that their disagreements are as worthy of our attention as their agreements, partly because we still live with similar disagreements among ourselves now.

Let’s lay out some of those key differences. Arendt drew deeply on Roman and Hebrew traditions, as well as on Aristotle; Weil rejected both the Roman and Hebrew traditions almost entirely, viewing them as intrinsically imperial and tainted by a love of violent and dominating power. Arendt, notwithstanding her lifelong intellectual friendship with Saint Augustine, rejected divine transcendence as the starting point for ethics, politics and citizenship. Arendt thought, instead, that shaping ethics according to transcendence ultimately produced an other-worldliness that drained citizenship of its urgency and, hence, its characteristic power to conserve and innovate in the world.

Weil, by contrast, believed that only by beginning with transcendence could we truly set ourselves free to act in the world; that rootedness in history begins with that which transcends and renews all roots. Hence her famous aphorism, ironically deeply Kabbalistic, that *the tree is really rooted in the sky*. In Weil’s view, only when we acknowledge createdness — that we did not make ourselves and cannot achieve anything aside from divine dependence — would we make any real moral, economic or political progress.

The two philosophers also view work and labour in quite different ways. Arendt follows a largely Greek worldview, setting out a hierarchy of labour, work and action, with biological labour constituting the lowest value and action to shape a common life constituting the highest of these values. By contrast, Weil viewed labour and work as integrally connected, seeing the physicality of basic labour as a way to be rooted in and connected to the created world, and the creator. Necessity grounds the human vocation and humbles our vocation as material, embodied humans. Through the life of necessity – basic biological life and the labour it entails — we learn forms of basic attention to what lies outside our own wills, and this is a sacred formation. Perhaps one reason for this difference is that Arendt has rejected createdness and grace from her account, while Weil has embraced both. It will be obvious that, in this regard, Weil is much closer to the Catholic tradition.

So, in an important sense, Arendt and Weil disagree about how we make a beginning and how we set our compasses towards meaningful participation in the world, toward love and justice.

What, then, is the productive basis of reading them together? What is shared? Both women shared a sense that modernity is characterised by the devaluation of the human person by systems of trade, representation and technocratic mastery. Both viewed modernity as fundamentally conflicted about questions of human value, marked by the paradox of the apparent high value of the human person that ultimately betrayed itself in its systems, both ideological and practical.

Particularly striking in this regard is the shared language of *uprootedness* used to describe modernity’s economic and political systems. Arendt believed that the category of the social had displaced that of the public, breeding individuals simultaneously private and endlessly “socialised” but unfit for the fundamentally public, civic tasks of building enduring cultures, institutions and movements. The loss of the public dimensions of our lives, eclipsed by the social, uproots us, breeding the paradox of social creatures who are fundamentally isolated creatures. This isolation, which is a form of deep loneliness, breeds the conditions for totalitarianism and authoritarianism, and for a susceptibility to political lies and fictions. For Weil, modern life fundamentally uproots us from modes of living in relationship to God, to culture, to land and to neighbour. Uprooted people, Weil thinks, respond to their uprootedness in two main ways: either they become inert and lethargic (her diagnosis of mid-century France), or else uprootedness drives us to uproot others (the diagnosis of Germany).

Both women were among the earliest critics of emerging ideas about human rights. Neither was against human rights *per se*, but both thought twentieth-century rights were built on misguided or fundamentally unstable foundations. Weil thought any notion of human rights without a prior account of human obligations was incoherent, and that rights language was primarily a language of trade or commerce, an extension of property thinking and not of fundamental human protection. Arendt thought post-war rights frameworks failed to deal with the basic conundrum that the right to have rights is largely predicated on citizenship, leaving those most in need of rights without meaningful access. 

Both women were also sharp observers of the tendency of liberal modernity, having wrapped itself in the language of restraint, to enact force and domination. Both also proposed that thinking within human limits is ethically generative.

Both can also be read as concerned about the development of forms of technological automation. Both women view modern technologies as inextricably bound up with a logic of technocratic mastery that tends to abstract us from a sense of the earth as a common world. Ardent, according to [Margaret Canovan](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/hannah-arendt/28CAEFF586687AEE04A4D72DF972341C), viewed modern technological development as tending towards maximising human power and minimising human responsibilities, risking a technological impulse that manifests a denial of the human. We manufacture our own denial. To be human, for Arendt, is to have the power to shape the world and to bear an inalienable responsibility for that power; to be humanly alive is to actively claim both that power and that responsibility.

For Weil, on the other hand, to be human is to pass as much as possible into the life of God, to dethrone the sovereign self, so that God acts more and more in us and so we toward love. But she is severe about love: there is no escape into love, only a passage to love through a confrontation with embodied suffering and injustice. Only the person who has looked injustice in the face and refuses to obey it can speak of the love and truth of the Gospel, she would write.

In this way, I think we can say that both women promote a version of a political anthropology of care — a non-sentimental ethics of care — as a form of resistance to an anthropology of mastery, force, domination or surveillance. And both view Jesus as a moral figure who exemplifies a kind of goodness that involves an almost total self-forgetting, and a capacity not only to love goodness but to love enemies. Thus the key contribution of Christianity to public life, for Arendt, is Christ’s doctrine of forgiveness.

Finally, neither woman is a straightforwardly liberal thinker. Both women occupied liminal positions with regard to liberal modernity, neither confessionally liberal nor post-liberal. They each thread together pre-modern and liberal-era thinkers to create exactly the “thinking in three tenses” I referred to earlier — but without nostalgia and without any sense that the past can be restored. They think with the paradox of broken threads.

These disagreements and shared purposes are my reasons to read Hannah Arendt and Simone Weil together now, in times that resonate with many of these same concerns. This leaves the question about why read them in dialogue with the Catholic social tradition. I will focus here on Arendt before turning to Weil on a later occasion.

First, despite her struggles with Christian politics, Arendt worked with a high doctrine of the human person. While she rejected transcendence as too otherworldly a place to begin, her rejection was partly predicated on a desire to defend, above all, human freedom and our capacity to shape the world in an enduring way. Her central ethical concern was to ensure the world’s endurance as a place fit for dignified habitation. By “world”, Arendt means something akin to Saint Paul and Saint Augustine: a common reality in space and time that we make out of our simple aliveness as conscious human beings and as those capable of being co-creators, those who “make” something intentional from time and space, a making which is inherently also a sharing with others. We make something of space and time by acting — by the activity of citizens that enables a diversity of peoples to form something truly common, something that gives expression to the blessing of our plurality and the gift of our freedom. For Arendt, the rational and hopeful shaping of this world is a uniquely human task and intrinsic to any meaningful concept of human dignity.

And yet, for Arendt, we are paradoxical beings. Too often we use our power — our capacity to make and to act — against our true selves. We act using those uniquely human capacities in order to undermine our own flourishing. We invent ever more complex mechanisms that draw on the excellence of our human capacities – our mental and technical abilities –to betray our deepest selves. As [Margaret Canovan](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/hannah-arendt/28CAEFF586687AEE04A4D72DF972341C) puts it, we are driven to find ever new ways to pretend we are not human. If there is a notion proximate to social sin in her thought, it is this. The most harmful of these manufactured denials are the ones that seek to deny that we are plural and that we are free to begin again, even in the ruins. Arendt thought these two lay at the heart of most of the traumas and failures of the modern era.

Second, it is from Augustine that Arendt derives her focus on natality: the idea that our beginning, the miraculous fact that each of us was born, is as significant a determinant of our sense of being in the world as the fact that we will die. Arendt thought the existential philosophies of her era made too much of our gaze towards death and mortality, and too little of the miracle of our basic aliveness that theologians had perhaps been better at honouring. In [*The Human Condition*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/H/bo29137972.html), Arendt defined natality as follows:

The miracle that saves the world, the realm of human affairs, from its normal, “natural” ruin is ultimately the fact of natality, in which the faculty of action is ontologically rooted. It is, in other words, the birth of new men and the new beginning, the action they are capable of by virtue of being born.


Arendt’s view is that if you want a high doctrine of the realm of human action and its world-creating, world-sharing potential, you need to focus properly on humans as natal and mortal. The continuous cycle of the birth of human beings imbued with freedom, judgement and the capacity to take responsibility for the world means that a new beginning can, not only may, be made.

Natality is also linked to a further key contribution to political theory that resonates with Catholic social thought. Arendt’s other beloved Augustinian sentiment — though one he probably never uttered — was the aphorism, “I want that you may be”. It is ethical to desire the being, the enduring being, of another creature. If our fate in death is the same, our existence by dint of our birth is not; it is marked by difference and by the spectre of a plural world. For Arendt, plurality is the proper name for social difference. It enables us to speak meaningfully and ethically about the “givenness” of difference among a community of others.

One of Arendt’s most theologically resonant moments appears in her orientation toward the most significant marker of her own difference, a politically scripted difference: her Jewishness. Arendt [wrote to Gershom Sholem in Jerusalem](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo14351141.html) to express her view that the correct disposition toward difference — plurality that is “given and not made” — is simply gratitude:

to be a Jew belongs for me to the indisputable facts of my life, and I have never had the wish to change or disdain facts of this kind. There is such a thing as a basic gratitude for everything that is as it is; for what has been given and not made.


This is a striking statement in Arendt’s work, because she is generally a non-foundationalist thinker who does not believe in a natural political order: for her, we negotiate, through the life of action, fitting political orders. The best orders are those that protect plurality, judgement, responsibility and natality, the freedom to act so that the world endures.

Gratitude is the appropriate response to the givenness of difference, which is to be treated as a blessing. Politics honours that blessing, or else it is *anti-politics*. Plural politics emerges from an almost theological disposition toward the blessing of what we cannot make, that which lies before action. The logic flows from givenness to recognition of the blessing, to gratitude, to political bestowing: of rights and reciprocal duties, and a logic of care. This is a pretty good antidote to the roots of a politics of fear.

Arendt drew this from a fusion of her reading of Augustine on free will and from Jewish sources. Her [dissertation](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo3619601.html) includes a paragraph in which she made these connections more explicit — between natality, memory, plurality and gratitude as ethical categories she wished to think with, all of which were intrinsically linked in her mind. She wrote:

Gratitude for life having been given at all is the spring of remembrance and gratitude: “Giving thanks for wanting to be as you are that you may be delivered from an existence that you do not want. For you are willing to be and unwilling to be miserable” [Augustine]. This willingness to be under all circumstances is the hallmark of man’s attachment to the transmundane source of his existence.


Much of Arendt’s work was focused on the ethical language of conditions: the human condition; the pre-political conditions that make a healthy politics possible; the condition of a philosophy that does not betray its search for truth and become the handmaiden of injustice.

It is striking that the other body of social thought that shares a focus on this language of “conditions” is Catholic social thought. The Second Vatican Council [adopts a definition of the common good](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_two/article_2/ii_the_common_good.html) focused on exactly this language of conditions: as “the sum total of social conditions which allow people, either as groups or as individuals, to reach their fulfilment more fully and more easily”. It remains now to deepen this dialogue between Hannah Arendt, Simone Weil and the tradition of Catholic social thought concerning what, exactly, these conditions that foster goods truly human and genuinely common.
