## In This Article

Motivational interviewing helps people talk themselves into change instead of digging in when you push. Here are 7 techniques you can use at work, in coaching and at home.

Devon had the same conversation with his employee three Mondays in a row. Be more proactive. Take initiative. Own your projects. Each time, he leaned in a little harder, and each time the employee nodded, said the right things and then changed nothing. By the third Monday Devon was convinced the guy just didn’t care.

Then a coach asked Devon one question: “What happens right after you tell him what to do?”

Devon thought about it. The employee got quiet. Defensive. Slower, if anything.

That’s the trap almost every manager, parent and coach walks into. When you push someone toward change, they often push back — not because they disagree, but because being told what to do threatens their sense of control. Psychologists call this reaction<sup>[1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4675534/)</sup> psychological reactance, and it’s why “just try harder to convince them” so often does the opposite.

Motivational interviewing takes the other road. Instead of supplying the reasons to change, you help the other person find their own. Here’s how it works, and seven techniques you can start using this week.

## What Is Motivational Interviewing?

What is motivational interviewing? Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative style of conversation that strengthens a person’s own motivation and commitment to change. Instead of telling someone what to do, you guide them to talk through why they might want to change and how they’d go about it.

It was originally developed<sup>[2](https://www.umass.edu/studentlife/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/Motivational_Interviewing_Definition_Principles_Approach.pdf)</sup> in counseling settings in the 1980s, and the research on it has since spread far beyond the clinic. A large research review<sup>[3](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1049731509347850?journalCode=rswa)</sup> that pooled more than a hundred studies found the effects are usually modest, but they’re consistent and they tend to last, and they hold up across a wide range of settings, from schools to workplaces. Worth being clear-eyed about: MI is a low-key nudge rather than a magic wand, and it works roughly as well as more directive approaches while feeling far less like a fight. Teachers use it to re-engage students. Managers use it in coaching conversations. Parents use it with teenagers who go silent the second they hear a lecture coming.

The idea underneath all of it is simple: change that comes from inside a person sticks better than change forced from outside.

MI rests on a few core commitments. Think of them as the mindset behind the moves:

1. **Partnership** : You work*with* the person, not on them. You’re two experts in the room — you on the topic, them on their own life.
2. **Acceptance** : You respect their worth and their right to make their own choices, even choices you wouldn’t make.
3. **Compassion** : You’re on their side, genuinely working for their benefit.
4. **Evocation** : You draw motivation out of them rather than pouring it in.

The practical skills of MI spell the acronym **OARS**:

1. **Open questions** : Questions that invite the person to think out loud instead of answering yes or no.
2. **Affirmations** : Genuine recognition of their strengths and efforts.
3. **Reflections** : Active listening — saying back what you heard so they feel understood.
4. **Summaries** : Pulling together what they’ve shared to show you’re tracking with them.

Below, we’ll walk through how to put OARS and the MI mindset to work in any setting.

## The Mindset Behind Motivational Interviewing

Before the techniques, three principles set the tone for every MI conversation. Get these wrong and the tactics fall flat. Get them right and the tactics almost run themselves.

### 1. Collaboration

The heart of MI is collaboration, where both people work toward a shared goal instead of one lecturing the other. Rather than positioning yourself as the expert with all the answers, you build a partnership.

To keep the conversation collaborative:

- Treat the other person as an equal partner in the change rather than a problem to fix.
- Regularly acknowledge what they bring — their insights, their strengths, the good ideas they raise. The affirmations below are a great way to do this.
- Keep your body language open and approachable. Sit at the same level as the other person, and skip [confrontational body language](/condescending-body-language/) like looming over them or using a steepling[hand gesture](/hand-gestures/) .
- Talk to them like a peer. Speaking too slowly or over-explaining can come across as talking down.
- Loosely [mirror their posture](/mirroring/) . If they’re relaxed, ease up too; if they’re sitting up and engaged, match that energy.

### 2. Empathy

Empathy creates the safe space someone needs to explore what’s really holding them back. It’s the art of making a person feel understood and cared for, and it’s the fastest route to [building rapport](/build-rapport-with-clients/).

It doesn’t matter whether you’re coaching a client on time management or nudging a friend toward a goal they keep abandoning. If they don’t feel understood, they won’t budge.

To show empathy in an MI conversation:

- Practice [active listening](/how-to-stop-talking-so-much/) by leaning in, making eye contact, nodding and using small verbal cues like “mhmm” or “I hear you.” Never glance at your phone. It tells them you don’t really care.
- Validate the feeling before you touch the behavior. Try “I can see why you’d feel that way” or “It makes sense you’d be frustrated.”
- Resist the urge to fix. Sometimes the most useful thing you can do is prove you understand the problem before you go anywhere near a solution.

Want to sharpen this skill? Here are [The 15 Habits of Highly Empathetic People (Empathy Guide)](/empathy/).

### 3. Autonomy

Remember Devon’s employee going quiet every time he got a fresh set of marching orders? That’s autonomy under threat. People protect their sense of freedom, and when they feel it slipping, resistance shows up — sometimes as arguing, sometimes as silence.

In MI, honoring autonomy is non-negotiable. The whole method is built on drawing out the person’s own arguments for change rather than supplying your own.

Encourage autonomy by:

- **Honoring their choices out loud.** Something like: “You know your life better than I ever will. Setting aside everyone else’s opinions, what do*you* actually want here?”
- **Offering options instead of orders.** If you want an employee to reconnect with their motivation, try: “You’ve always been drawn to creative work. What kind of project would make you excited to log on Monday?”
- **Helping them weigh their own plan.** If someone decides to ease into a change gradually rather than all at once, talk through the upsides and the trade-offs of their approach — not yours.
- **Leaving room to adjust.** Let them know the plan can flex as they go. MI favors personal, adaptable goals over rigid ones.

Your job is to make it clear you believe in this person’s ability to change. The change should line up with their [personal values](/core-values/) and [goals](/smart-goals/) rather than something bolted on from the outside.

The best motivator you’ll ever have in a conversation is the other person’s own reason for change. Your job is just to help them hear it out loud.


## The Four Processes: How an MI Conversation Unfolds

MI isn’t a script you run top to bottom. It moves through four processes, and you’ll often loop back through them as the conversation shifts. Think of them less as steps and more as gears you shift between.

1. **Engaging** —*“Are we walking together?”* Before anything else, you build a real working relationship. No agenda, no fixing, just enough trust that the person feels safe being honest. Skip this and everything after it stalls.
2. **Focusing** —*“Where are we headed?”* You and the other person settle on what you’re actually here to talk about. The key word is*together* — you agree on the focus instead of you deciding it for them.
3. **Evoking** —*“Why would you want this?”* This is the heart of MI: drawing out the person’s own motivation. It’s where change talk lives (more on that below), and where your reflections and open questions do the most work.
4. **Planning** —*“So what’s the first step?”* Only once someone actually sounds ready do you move to a concrete, flexible plan — one they build and own rather than one you hand them.

The order matters. Jump straight to planning — “here’s what you should do” — before someone is engaged and voicing their own reasons, and you’re right back to pushing. Which, as Devon learned, is exactly what triggers the resistance we started with.

## 7 Motivational Interviewing Techniques For Any Setting

Principles are nice, but you need moves you can actually use with your clients, students, team or family. Here are seven.

### 1. Ask open-ended questions

The first move in MI is to ask questions that get the other person thinking out loud about their own strengths, values and options. Compared with yes/no questions, open questions are shown to draw out<sup>[4](https://journals.lww.com/ajnonline/Abstract/2007/10000/Motivational_Interviewing__An_evidence_based.30.aspx)</sup> deeper, more detailed<sup>[5](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6538818/)</sup> answers.

If you have a specific change in mind — say you want an employee to take more initiative so they can grow into a bigger role — ask questions that lead *them* to see the upside. The goal is for the change to feel like their idea, because ultimately it’s their call.

| Open-Ended Questions (Ask These!) | Closed Questions (Avoid These!) | 
|---|---|
| What are the pros and cons of changing this? | Would changing this be good for you? | 
| Can you tell me more about how you’re feeling here? | Do you feel overwhelmed? | 
| How would reaching this goal change your day-to-day? | Would this improve your daily life? | 
| What matters most to you in this situation? | Do you want to improve? | 
| What steps could get you closer to where you want to be? | Would daily check-ins help? | 
| What do you think you could do differently? | Did you make a mistake? | 

Here’s more on [How to Ask Open-Ended Questions That Spark Good Conversation](/open-ended-questions/).

**Pro Tip:** When you catch yourself about to give advice, turn it into a question instead. “Have you thought about blocking your mornings?” becomes “When do you do your best focused work?” The second one hands them the steering wheel.

### 2. Set collaborative goals

Collaborative goals means you build the target *together*, so it fits their values and their readiness to change. Watch how a manager coaches a team member on customer-service skills:

- **Manager** : “Renata, I really value how committed you are to getting better at this. Let’s set a couple of goals together that fit what you want and what the team needs. Where would you like to focus?”
- **Employee** : “I think my communication slips when I’m dealing with a difficult customer. I get flustered and can’t find the right words.”
- **Manager** : “That’s a great thing to target — it’s exactly where the pressure’s highest. What would you like to get better at specifically?”
- **Employee** : “I’d love techniques for active listening and calming a tense situation. And ways to actually connect with the customer so we land on something that works for both of us.”
- **Manager** : “Love it. Let’s break that into small steps. What if we start with some active-listening and de-escalation practice, run a few role-plays, then check in regularly on how it’s going? How does that sound?”

Notice the “let’s” and “we.” The goal helps both sides: the company gets a stronger team member, and Renata moves toward her own [professional development goals](/professional-development-goals/).

**Action Step:** In your next coaching or feedback conversation, open with “Where would *you* like to focus?” before you say a word about where you think they should focus.

### 3. Send positive nonverbal cues

In MI, [body language](/body-language-important/) does quiet, constant work — building rapport, showing empathy and keeping the person at ease. Stay aware of your own cues and read theirs.

These [therapeutic communication techniques](/therapeutic-communication/) help build trust fast:

- Lean slightly forward to show you’re engaged and interested.
- Get on their level. Standing over someone puts you in a power position that can shut the conversation down.
- Hold [warm eye contact](/making-eye-contact/) to show you’re paying attention, but don’t lock on. Glancing away naturally every few seconds keeps it comfortable.
- Share a little about yourself when it fits, so you come across as a real person who can relate rather than an authority running an evaluation.

### 4. Elicit change talk

This is where MI really works. “Change talk” is any statement where the person voices their own desire, ability, reason or need to change. The more they say it, the more likely they are to actually do it, so your job is to fish for it and then reflect it back.

Say you want a team member to manage their time better. Instead of listing the reasons they should, ask: “What are some reasons *you’d* want to get on top of your schedule?”

They might answer: “Honestly, better time management would cut my stress and I’d stop letting my teammates down when I run late.” That’s change talk. They just made your argument for you, in their own words.

Change talk comes in a few flavors, and the later ones are the strongest sign someone’s about to move. An easy way to spot them:

- **Desire** : “I*want* to get more organized.”
- **Ability** : “I*could* probably block my mornings.”
- **Reasons** : “It’d cut my stress and I’d stop letting the team down.”
- **Need** : “I*have* to fix this — it’s not sustainable.”
- **Commitment** : “I’m*going* to start Monday.”
- **Activation** : “I’m*ready* to give it a shot.”
- **Taking steps** : “I already blocked tomorrow morning.”

The first four show someone warming up to the idea. The last three mean they’re actually moving: those are the ones to slow down on and reinforce hardest.

The flip side is **sustain talk**: everything that argues for staying put, like “I don’t really have time” or “I’ve tried before and it never sticks.” Here’s the useful part: what predicts real change is the *balance* between change talk and sustain talk. So your job runs two ways: draw out more of the first, and stop provoking the second (which is exactly what happens when you argue the case for them).

**Try this:** When you hear change talk, don’t rush past it. Slow down and ask them to say more — “Tell me about the stress part.” Every extra sentence they spend describing their own reasons deepens the commitment.

### 5. Affirm and validate

People with low confidence tend to have less drive<sup>[6](https://www.proquest.com/openview/25a276f598ec54906172f017b84c8002/1?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=1819280)</sup> to pursue change on their own — they feel held back before they start. Affirmations counter that. Genuine ones, anchored in something real, [build the confidence](/how-to-be-more-confident/) a person needs to move.

Throughout the conversation, name their strengths:

- Achievements: “Weren’t you the top performer on the team last quarter? You’ve got a real track record here.”
- Obstacles they’ve cleared: “You’ve handled tougher things than this. I have zero doubt you’ve got the grit to make it happen.”
- Effort: “I can see how much work you’ve already put into this.”
- Growth: “Your communication has come a long way — you’re clearly self-aware and serious about improving.”

Keep affirmations tied to the change at hand, and keep them honest. Empty flattery breeds suspicion; real recognition earns respect.

### 6. Practice active and reflective listening

Active listening is shown to build<sup>[7](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1435506)</sup> rapport and trust — and in MI it does double duty, because reflecting back what someone says helps *them* hear their own thinking more clearly.

Show the speaker you’re fully with them. Here’s the difference:

| Active Listening | Distracted (Passive) Listening | 
|---|---|
| Steady eye contact | Darting eyes or checking your phone | 
| Nodding and reacting | Blank stare, no movement | 
| Facing toward them | Body angled away | 
| Reflecting back what they said | No response or a sudden topic change | 

**Pro Tip:** A reflection isn’t a parrot. Instead of repeating their exact words, offer the feeling underneath: “So it sounds like the hard part is figuring out where to start, more than the deadline itself.” When you name the thing they couldn’t quite name, they feel understood at a whole new level.

### 7. Provide summaries

Every so often, gather up what you’ve heard and hand it back. A summary is really just a longer reflection. You pull together the concerns, hopes and hesitations the person has shared and mirror them so they feel truly heard.

For example:

- They say: “I feel intimidated by the people ahead of me.” You reflect: “So the competition itself is what’s getting to you.”
- They say: “I keep failing to stick to a routine.” You reflect: “It’s less about wanting to and more about making it stick day to day.”
- They say: “I don’t know how I’ll get through the work event without a drink — I get so anxious.” You reflect: “If I’ve got this right, the anxious part is socializing without that familiar crutch.”

Reflective summaries prove you’re listening for real, and they help you see the situation through the other person’s eyes.

**Key caveat:** Don’t over-summarize. Do it too often and the person feels patronized. Use it a couple of times per conversation, and check your read with a light “Did I get that right?” or “Am I understanding you correctly?”

## Common Traps That Shut Change Talk Down

Even with the best intentions, a handful of reflexes will quietly sink an MI conversation. Watch for these:

- **The righting reflex.** The urge to jump in and fix, correct, or set someone straight. It feels helpful, but the second you start arguing for change, the other person starts arguing against it. Catch the reflex and ask a question instead.
- **The question-answer trap.** Firing off a run of closed questions turns a conversation into an interrogation. A good rule of thumb: follow most questions with a reflection rather than another question.
- **The expert trap.** Sliding into “I’ve seen this a hundred times, here’s what you should do.” It puts you above the person and invites them to go passive. Offer information lightly, then ask what they make of it.
- **The labeling trap.** Pinning a label on someone — “underperformer,” “unmotivated” — makes them defend the label instead of exploring the behavior. Stick to specifics.
- **The blaming trap.** Getting snagged on whose fault it is. Blame lives in the past; MI keeps its eyes on what the person wants to do next.
- **The chat trap.** Warm small talk that never actually goes anywhere. A little rapport is good, but gently steer toward what you’re both there to work on.

Notice the thread running through all six: every one of them quietly pulls control back to you. MI works by keeping the other person in the driver’s seat.

## Motivational Interviewing In Action: 3 Case Studies

If you’re wondering what MI looks like on the ground, here are three examples from different settings.

### Workplace: employee performance

Jasmine keeps missing performance expectations, and her manager wants to understand why before jumping to fixes. Using MI:

- The manager helps Jasmine picture what success would actually look like for her and maps it into a [professional development plan](/professional-development-plan/) .
- Together they name what’s getting in the way — [workplace gossip](/workplace-gossip/) pulling her attention, a dull office with no natural light draining her energy.
- Jasmine admits she’s been low on confidence since a recent project and could use more guidance.

By staying curious and non-judgmental, the manager helps Jasmine spot her own strengths, set goals she believes in and build a plan she’s bought into. Her performance climbs, and so does her confidence.

### Coaching: a stalled career

Marcus, a mid-career designer, tells his mentor he feels stuck but “doesn’t know what he wants.” Instead of prescribing a five-year plan, the mentor uses MI:

- She asks open questions about the moments Marcus has felt most alive at work, and the moments he’s dreaded.
- She reflects a pattern back: the energy shows up on hands-on creative projects and drains away in status meetings.
- She affirms the risks he’s taken before and asks what a bolder next step might look like.

Marcus talks himself toward pitching a new role built around the work he loves. Because it was his idea, he actually follows through.

### Education: a disengaged student

John has checked out of school — no interest, no effort. His teacher tries MI:

- She asks how John feels about his classes, what grabs him and what bores him stiff.
- They explore the quiet worry underneath: he feels like he’s letting his parents down and doesn’t know how to turn it around.
- Knowing John loves sports, the teacher connects effort in class to the discipline he already admires in athletes.
- They celebrate a real win — his recent jump from C’s to B’s — and set up a study group with classmates he likes.

Through collaborative goals and regular check-ins, John re-engages, his grades rise and his confidence follows.

## Key Takeaways: Help People Change By Drawing Out Their Own Reasons

Motivational interviewing works because it flips the usual script. Instead of arguing someone into change, you help them argue themselves into it, and that kind of change lasts.

To do it well:

- Ask open questions that get people thinking out loud and reaching their own conclusions.
- Affirm real strengths and effort so they feel capable of change.
- Listen actively and reflect back what you hear, so they feel genuinely understood.
- Summarize now and then to show you’re tracking, and to help them hear their own thinking.
- Above all, protect their autonomy. The moment change feels imposed, people dig in. The moment it feels like their choice, they move.

Try one technique in your next hard conversation — just the open questions, or just reflecting back what you hear. You’ll be surprised how much more a person opens up when they feel like the author of their own change. You’ve got this.
